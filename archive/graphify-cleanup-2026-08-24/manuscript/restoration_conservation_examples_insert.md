# Manuscript insert — restoration and conservation as the primary ecological arc

> Intended role: replace island/urban as the primary empirical illustration. These cases do not prove the finite CREST theorem; they show that its qualitative carrier/state/evidence/target separation recurs across independent restoration domains.

## Restoration changes what must be known

Ecological restoration provides a particularly direct setting for CREST because restoration is explicitly intervention-driven. A degraded ecosystem can admit one coarse description while a management action is unavailable, yet require a finer state once dam removal, invasive-species control, rewetting, prescribed burning, reintroduction, or another intervention becomes part of the future the representation must support. The key empirical question is not simply whether treatment effects are heterogeneous. It is whether worlds treated as the same before intervention diverge under the same newly relevant action.

The 2026 Society for Ecological Restoration standards already separate baseline assessment, goals and objectives, implementation, indicators, effectiveness monitoring, impact monitoring, and adaptive management (Gann et al. 2026). CREST does not claim novelty for those distinctions. It adds a state-representation question: **does the intervention make a previously erased ecological difference necessary for prediction, monitoring, or reporting?**

### Dam removal: feasible futures expand before full recovery is known

The Elwha River is a strong example of the first CREST layer. Two dams had blocked anadromous fish from more than 90% of the watershed. Their removal reopened upstream habitat and therefore enlarged the set of ecologically feasible migration and recolonization trajectories. Post-removal eDNA monitoring using 11 species-specific assays showed that most targeted anadromous species moved above both former dam sites, but recolonization timing and spatial extent differed among species (Duda et al. 2021). Thus `barrier removed` is not itself an adequate post-restoration ecological state. Once passage is possible, species identity, life history, migration timing and habitat response can become distinctions that a predictive state must retain. Nevertheless, a coarse target such as whether a focal salmon species has passed a former barrier can remain reportable without complete identification of the restored community state.

### Invasive-species control: a target can be achieved while restoration state remains unresolved

Invasive-plant removal makes the evidence/target distinction especially visible. Flory and Clay (2009) achieved 87–99% *Microstegium vimineum* biomass reduction, but native-diversity responses depended on removal method. In a 4.5-year *Acacia longifolia* removal study, soil microbial measures recovered faster than carbon and nitrogen pools, and recovery depended on invasion history and litter removal (Marchante et al. 2009). Rohal et al. (2021) found that above-ground vegetation generally reflected seed-bank communities after *Phragmites* treatment, but also found response-relevant mismatches and insufficient native seed banks at degraded sites. Thus `invader controlled` can be licensed by abundance monitoring while `native ecosystem recovered` remains unresolved in some systems. CREST treats these as different report targets, while rejecting a universal claim that above-ground monitoring always fails to track below-ground state.

### Peatland rewetting: the same hydrological action can require a different state for a different target

Peatland rewetting shows why restoration state is target-relative. After 7–16 years of rewetting, methanogen abundance and community composition approached pristine conditions while methane-production activity remained limited by substrate availability and incomplete vegetation recovery (Urbanová and Bárta 2020). A 7.5-year, 4,800-ha tropical peatland trial likewise monitored water level, peat subsidence and tree regrowth as distinct response dimensions (Hooijer et al. 2024). Hydrological success therefore does not automatically identify biogeochemical or vegetation recovery. A state sufficient for the target `water table restored` can be too coarse for the target `carbon-cycle function restored`.

### Independent recurrence in prescribed fire

Fire restoration provides an independent but partial replication of the same motif. Varner et al. (2007) assigned 16 longleaf-pine stands that had been fire-excluded for about 40 years to three duff-moisture burn treatments or an unburned control. Dry burns produced 20.5% mean pine mortality, whereas wet and moist burns did not differ from the control; duff consumption and crown scorch predicted mortality. Thus a vegetation-only description omitting accumulated duff and its moisture can be inadequate once `burn` enters the future grammar. This experiment directly supports the action-by-hidden-history response link, but it did not also test the full evidence-state versus reportable-target chain.

Across freshwater restoration, invasion management, peatland restoration and fire ecology, the evidence supports the repeated projection

\[
\boxed{
\text{new restoration capability}
\rightarrow
\text{new response-relevant distinctions}
\rightarrow
\text{finer required state}
\rightarrow
\text{possible monitoring inadequacy},
}
\]

while a coarser management target can remain reportable. No cited study was designed to test this whole chain end to end. The intervention responses are directly observed; the required-state and reportability links are CREST interpretations of those results and of the stated management targets. The cases therefore support a cross-domain qualitative projection, not an empirical replication of the finite theorem or its arbitrary scaling. The finite theorem alone shows why no carrier-gain-only bound on representational burden exists without additional structural assumptions.
