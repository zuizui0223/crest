## Introduction / prior-art paragraph

Predictive-state approaches provide a direct formal antecedent to CREST's temporal language. Computational mechanics groups histories into causal states when they induce the same conditional distribution over futures (Shalizi & Crutchfield, 2001). Predictive State Representations go further for controlled systems: Littman, Sutton and Singh represent state by multi-step, action-conditional predictions of future observations, and later PSR work represents histories through predictions of observable outcomes of future tests that could be performed on the system (Littman, Sutton & Singh, 2002; Singh, James & Rudary, 2004). CREST therefore does not claim novelty for defining state by predictive equivalence, for allowing future experiments to determine present predictive state, or for replacing hidden state with future-test predictions. Its narrower contribution is to externalize several ecology-specific responsibilities—future/composition, inherited semantics, retained mechanisms, carrier feasibility, evidence licensing, and report target—and to study their coupled consequences for one ecological state-equivalence relation.

## Snapshot-sufficiency paragraph

The implication

\[
X(\omega)=X(\omega')\Rightarrow q_{\mathcal C,V}(\omega)=q_{\mathcal C,V}(\omega')
\]

is a **factorization criterion**, not a novelty-bearing theorem: it says that the required state map factors through the present-snapshot map. CREST's theorem-level content lies in finite conditions, obstructions and cross-gate results that determine when such a coarse representation is or is not adequate under the declared ecological contract.

## Positioning against PSR/POMDP

A sufficiently rich PSR or POMDP can encode many of the same latent variables, action contingencies and observation uncertainties used by CREST. CREST does not claim non-embeddability or greater expressive power. The proposed distinction is architectural: CREST makes separately inspectable scientific obligations and failure certificates out of future/composition sufficiency, inherited-semantic portability, retained-mechanism robustness, carrier feasibility, evidence identification and target reportability. The finite cross-gate witnesses then show how those obligations can move in different directions when the contract changes. This is a decomposition and adequacy claim, not a claim that generic predictive-state models cannot reproduce the same dynamics after suitable augmentation.
