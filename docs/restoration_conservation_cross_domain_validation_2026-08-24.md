# CREST restoration/conservation cross-domain validation — 2026-08-24

> Current empirical mainline: test whether the same **capability → required-state → evidence → target** separation recurs across independent restoration and conservation domains. Island/urban examples remain supplementary and are no longer the primary ecological arc.

## Central restoration claim

Restoration changes more than the ecosystem. By making a new future action scientifically relevant, it can also change which present distinctions must be retained as ecological state.

For an intervention `a`, a coarse present quotient `q0` fails for the restoration contract when

\[
q_0(\omega)=q_0(\omega')
\quad\text{but}\quad
R(\omega,a)\neq R(\omega',a).
\]

The empirical question is therefore not whether restoration responses are heterogeneous in general. It is whether **previously ignorable distinctions become response-relevant once a restoration action enters the future grammar**.

The finite CREST theorem remains separate: ecological case studies do not prove the arbitrary-`m` capability–resolution result.

---

## Restoration practice already separates targets, indicators and monitoring

The Society for Ecological Restoration (SER) 2026 International Principles and Standards, third edition, distinguishes assessment, planning/design, implementation, ongoing management, and monitoring/evaluation. It requires explicit goals, objectives and measurable indicators, and distinguishes implementation/compliance, effectiveness/performance, and impact monitoring. It also recommends intervention and untreated control plots where appropriate. DOI: `10.1111/rec.70441`.

CREST does not claim novelty for monitoring, adaptive management or purpose-specific indicators. The additional question is:

> **When does a newly admitted restoration action make the old present-state representation itself inadequate?**

# Case R1 — Dam removal: expanding the feasible ecological world set

### Intervention

Remove a migration barrier / restore longitudinal connectivity.

### Empirical anchor

Elwha dam removal restored access to habitat that had been inaccessible to anadromous fish for about a century. NOAA reports that the two dams had blocked access to more than 90% of the watershed, and post-removal monitoring tracks fish population levels, life-history diversity, growth, survival, food webs and other ecosystem responses.

Duda et al. used 11 species-specific eDNA assays after Elwha dam removal. Most targeted anadromous species passed upstream of both former dams, but recolonization timing and spatial extent differed among species; Chinook and Coho moved farther into the watershed than less abundant Pink and Chum salmon. DOI: `10.1002/edn3.134`.

The ongoing Klamath restoration programme similarly uses eDNA after four dam removals intended to reconnect more than 640 km of habitat and restore nearly 1,000 ha of former reservoir footprint.

### CREST four-layer mapping

1. **Feasible ecological worlds:** barrier removal enlarges the set of reachable migration, recolonization, gene-flow and food-web trajectories.
2. **Required state:** once passage is possible, species/stock identity, migration timing, life history, habitat use and native/non-native status can become response-relevant distinctions.
3. **Evidence-identified state:** eDNA and other monitoring identify only part of this response structure and do so with species- and method-specific uncertainty.
4. **Reportable target:** coarse targets such as `target species detected above former dam` can be reportable without identifying the full post-removal community state.

### CREST diagnosis

This is the strongest real-world analogue of Gate A. A small management grammar change — open one previously blocked connection — enlarges the future set and simultaneously increases the range of distinctions that monitoring may have to resolve.

### Evidence grade

**A.** Direct before/after ecosystem-scale intervention plus multi-year species-specific monitoring.

### Claim boundary

The real system does not establish `Δ|K*|=1` or an arbitrary `m`-bit burden. It establishes the qualitative motif: connectivity action → feasible-future expansion → species-specific response distinctions → expanded monitoring responsibility.

### Sources

- NOAA Fisheries, `Dam Removals on the Elwha River`, updated 2025.
- Duda et al. 2021, *Environmental DNA*, DOI `10.1002/edn3.134`.
- NOAA/PCSRF Klamath eDNA recolonization project, active 2025–2026.

# Case R2 — Invasive-plant removal: a reportable control target can leave the restoration state unresolved

### Intervention

Remove or suppress an invasive alien plant.

### Empirical anchor

A 440-study review of N2-fixing woody invaders identified persistent restoration barriers after invasive-species control: altered soil microbial communities, depleted native seed banks, elevated soil N, secondary invasion, reinvasion and dominance by weedy native species. DOI: `10.1111/rec.12669`.

A 2023 systematic review of native-community restoration after invasive-alien-plant management concluded that removal alone is often insufficient. More than 35% of reviewed studies reported reinvasion and/or secondary invasion, while only a small fraction examined seed-bank response. The review emphasizes invasive seed-bank reduction and propagule arrival from surrounding invaded areas. DOI: `10.1016/j.ecoleng.2023.107122`.

### CREST four-layer mapping

1. **Feasible ecological worlds:** removal reopens trajectories in which native vegetation can recover.
2. **Required state:** soil nutrient legacy, microbial state, native/invasive seed banks, propagule pressure and secondary invaders can become necessary distinctions for predicting recovery.
3. **Evidence-identified state:** above-ground vegetation monitoring can certify invader reduction while leaving below-ground recovery state unresolved.
4. **Reportable target:** `invader controlled/removed` can be reportable even when `native ecosystem recovered` is not.

### CREST diagnosis

This is the cleanest empirical case for

\[
\text{reportable management target}
\neq
\text{identified restoration state}.
\]

The action succeeds on one target while exposing a larger state problem downstream.

### Evidence grade

**A/B.** Large reviews plus long-term removal experiments; the motif is repeatedly documented across invasion systems.

### Claim boundary

CREST does not claim discovery of ecological legacy effects. The contribution is to say exactly when a legacy must enter the scientific state: when forgetting it merges worlds with different responses to the declared restoration action or target.

# Case R3 — Peatland rewetting: identical hydrological action, different hidden-history response

### Intervention

Block drainage / raise water table / rewet degraded peat.

### Empirical anchor

Long-term rewetting (7–16 years) can restore methanogen abundance and composition toward pristine conditions while methane-production activity remains low because vegetation and natural substrate supply have not fully recovered. DOI: `10.1016/j.ecoleng.2020.105852`.

A large-scale Sumatra restoration trial raised water levels over about 4,800 ha using 257 peat dams and monitored recovery for 7.5 years. Rewetting approximately halved peat-subsidence rates where water tables rose from roughly −0.6 m to −0.3 m, while vegetation recovery remained a separate restoration dimension. *Scientific Reports* 2024, article 10721.

A 2026 online study further reports that severe wildfire legacy can strongly alter methane response after rewetting, illustrating how historical disturbance may become response-relevant when rewetting is the action. DOI: `10.1016/j.ese.2026.100735`.

### CREST four-layer mapping

1. **Feasible ecological worlds:** rewetting makes anaerobic recovery, peat stabilization and peatland vegetation trajectories feasible again.
2. **Required state:** drainage history, vegetation/substrate state, microbial composition and — for climate targets — disturbance/fire history can become response-relevant.
3. **Evidence-identified state:** water-table recovery does not by itself identify microbial/process recovery or full greenhouse-gas state.
4. **Reportable target:** `hydrology restored` can be reportable while `carbon-climate function restored` remains unresolved.

### CREST diagnosis

This case gives a sharp target-relativity example:

\[
T_{\rm hydrology}
\neq
T_{\rm climate}
\]

and therefore the state sufficient for one restoration target need not be sufficient for the other.

### Evidence grade

**A/B.** Long-term rewetting comparisons and large-scale restoration trials.

# Case R4 — Prescribed-fire reintroduction: the action reveals dormant historical and below-ground distinctions

### Intervention

Reintroduce fire to a fire-dependent ecosystem after long exclusion.

### Empirical anchor

Long-unburned longleaf-pine restoration shows that reintroducing historically natural fire can generate novel, undesirable effects because long fire exclusion changes duff and fuel structure. Reviews report substantial old-tree mortality in some first-entry burns and identify deep duff/smouldering around old trees as a mechanism. DOI: `10.1111/j.1526-100x.2005.00067.x`; USDA synthesis DOI `10.2737/RMRS-GTR-238`.

Prescribed-fire effects on soil seed banks can also differ from effects on the visible above-ground community. A 2026 rangeland review explicitly treats these response domains as associated but distinct. DOI: `10.1016/j.rala.2026.06.002`.

In sagebrush ecosystems, seed-bank germination responses differ with season of prescribed fire and ecological condition. DOI: `10.1016/j.jaridenv.2013.04.001`.

### CREST four-layer mapping

1. **Feasible ecological worlds:** prescribed fire creates a new disturbance/recovery trajectory.
2. **Required state:** time since fire, duff/fuel structure, seed bank, invasive load, phenology and ecological condition become action-relevant distinctions.
3. **Evidence-identified state:** above-ground vegetation alone can fail to identify seed-bank or fuel-mediated response state.
4. **Reportable target:** `burn completed` or `fuel reduced` can be reportable while `restoration/resilience state` remains unresolved.

### CREST diagnosis

This is the clearest analogue of the sentence:

> **The ecosystem need not change first; making `burn` part of the scientific future can make previously ignored history and below-ground structure state-relevant.**

### Evidence grade

**A/B.** Experimental prescribed burns plus multi-system syntheses.

# Cross-domain recurrence

| Domain | Newly relevant capability | Previously ignorable distinction that can become state-relevant | Old evidence can remain sufficient for | But not necessarily for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| dam removal | open fish passage | species/stock, life history, migration timing, habitat response | barrier removal / focal detection | full recolonization state |
| invasive removal | remove invader | soil N, microbes, seed bank, propagule pressure | invader control | native-community recovery |
| peat rewetting | restore water table | drainage/fire history, vegetation, substrates, methanogens | hydrological recovery | carbon / methane recovery |
| prescribed fire | reintroduce burn | fire history, duff/fuel, seed bank, ecological condition | treatment/fuel target | regeneration / resilience state |

The recurring pattern is therefore not `restoration outcomes vary`. The stronger operational motif is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{new restoration capability}
\to
\text{new response-relevant distinctions}
\to
\text{required-state refinement}
\to
\text{possible monitoring inadequacy}
}
\]

while a coarser target can remain reportable.

This is the real-world interpretation of CREST's four-layer architecture. The finite theorem adds a no-bound existence result; the restoration cases show that the qualitative architecture recurs independently in freshwater, invasion, peatland and fire ecology.

# Mainline decision

## Promote to the manuscript-facing ecological arc

1. **Elwha/Klamath dam removal** — best Gate-A / feasible-futures example.
2. **Invasive-plant removal** — best identified-state versus reportable-target example.
3. **Peatland rewetting** — best history-dependent response and target-relativity example.

## Independent replication / support

4. **Prescribed fire** — strong confirmation that restoration actions expose latent historical/below-ground distinctions.

## Demote from primary arc

Island and urban cases remain useful application material and prospective validation systems, but are no longer the primary ecological narrative for the current CREST paper.

# Falsifiable restoration projections

CREST gains empirical value only if it generates discriminating comparisons. The next restoration tests should compare a deliberately coarse pre-intervention state against a response-aware state.

1. **Dam removal:** species/stock-aware state should outperform simple pre-removal presence/absence when predicting recolonization timing and extent.
2. **Invasive removal:** adding soil/seed-bank legacy information should improve prediction of native recovery beyond above-ground invader removal status.
3. **Rewetting:** hydrology-only state should be insufficient for methane/carbon recovery where vegetation/substrate/history differ; richer state should improve transportability.
4. **Prescribed fire:** above-ground vegetation-only state should be insufficient for post-burn regeneration where seed-bank/fuel-history differences are strong.

Failure of these richer states to improve the declared response task would weaken the ecological usefulness of the proposed CREST mapping, even though it would not refute the finite mathematical theorem.
