# CREST ecological projection

> **Purpose:** project the proved finite CREST mathematics back into ecology without turning interpretations into extra theorem families.

## 1. Ecological systems as adaptive, partially observed dynamical worlds

CREST does not require ecosystems to be deterministic, nor does it assume that evolution monotonically maximizes one global fitness function.

The ecology-facing picture is instead:

\[
\text{variation + stochasticity + interaction}
\quad+\quad
\text{context-dependent selection}
\]

acting in a system where organisms alter one another's environments and therefore one another's future selective conditions.

The result is a complex adaptive dynamical system in which present ecological observables can fail to summarize all history-, mechanism-, or intervention-relevant distinctions.

CREST asks which of those distinctions must be retained in a state representation for a declared scientific task.

## 2. A state is not automatically a snapshot

The current mathematics is finite and exact, but a latent CREST world may include coordinates for:

- relevant ecological history;
- present population/community configuration;
- hidden response mechanism or causal channel;
- possible future responses to declared actions;
- observation/evidence identity;
- the target being reported.

This motivates a temporally thick interpretation:

\[
\boxed{
\text{ecological state}
\approx
\text{scientifically licensed compression of a temporally extended possible world}.
}
\]

This is an interpretation of the current latent-world formalism, not yet a theorem about continuous trajectories.

A future snapshot-sufficiency theorem would ask whether a present observable map \(X\) satisfies

\[
X(u)=X(v)
\Rightarrow
[u]_J=[v]_J.
\]

If not, two worlds can look identical now yet require different ecological states because of history, latent mechanism, basin membership, or future response.

## 3. CCOC projection — open futures

CCOC contributes the principle

\[
\boxed{
\text{current functional equivalence}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{causal equivalence under an enlarged future}.
}
\]

Ecological examples include:

- a currently absent pollinator becomes available;
- a corridor reconnects a population;
- a rare colonizer arrives;
- a predator or pathogen reservoir becomes reachable;
- a restoration action enters the management repertoire;
- a dormant interaction becomes addressable after rewiring.

Two communities can therefore share one present macrostate yet need different states once the set of relevant future interactions changes.

The important ecological variable is not only the current interaction network, but also the **space of interactions that can become relevant under the declared future grammar**.

## 4. MLTR projection — history and structural change

MLTR contributes a different problem: whether an inherited ecological category remains exact after turnover or replacement.

Examples:

- `pollination maintained` before and after pollinator-guild replacement;
- the same resilience class before and after network rewiring;
- the same habitat-quality class before and after fragmentation/restoration;
- the same functional group after species turnover.

The state label may remain verbally available while the old merge becomes scientifically unsafe.

This motivates the ecological distinction:

\[
\boxed{
\text{same current descriptor}
\neq
\text{same inherited operational meaning}.
}
\]

History becomes state-relevant only when forgetting it changes a declared output, action, successor, or carried meaning.

## 5. MRM projection — latent mechanisms and fitness-relevant structure

MRM addresses hidden mechanism differences.

The same visible ecological state can be compatible with multiple causal explanations. CREST does not require full mechanism identity to be represented. Instead:

\[
\boxed{
\text{latent mechanism difference becomes a state difference only when it changes a required response.}
}
\]

A useful example comes from context-dependent fitness rules. Distinct selective mechanisms can generate the same observed trait gradient when environmental drivers are correlated. A decoupled environment or intervention can expose the difference.

Thus a rule discovered in one observational context can be an effective rule on a coarse quotient rather than a law of the full latent world.

This does not imply relativism. It means the rule's validity has a domain: the distinctions erased by the observational or scientific quotient must remain irrelevant inside that domain.

## 6. CED projection — observation changes the accessible state space

CED emphasizes that science observes a map of the world, not the latent world itself.

Let

\[
O_V:U\to Y_V
\]

be an observation map associated with viewpoint/design \(V\). Changing the measurement scheme can change which latent worlds remain observationally equivalent.

The CREST evidence gate then asks whether the observation partition induced by \(O_V\) is fine enough to identify \(J\).

This separates two questions:

1. **What distinctions does an adequate ecological state require?**
2. **Which of those distinctions can this observation scheme actually resolve?**

A different viewpoint does not change nature's underlying dynamics. It changes the quotient through which those dynamics are scientifically accessed.

## 7. Structural monitoring debt

The strongest current measurement implication is that a monitoring deficit may be structural.

Suppose

\[
W(z)=F(z)R(z)
\]

with reproductive channel \(F\) and recruitment/reachability channel \(R\). The two latent changes

\[
(aF,R)
\quad\text{and}\quad
(F,aR)
\]

have identical net performance \(aFR\).

Therefore any observation depending only on \(W\) preserves the causal symmetry. Repeating the same measurement with more precision or larger sample size does not identify which channel changed.

If an \(F\)-specific intervention becomes relevant, however, the two worlds can require different states.

The practical consequence is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{monitoring adequacy may require a new measurement type, not merely more replication.}
}
\]

This gives ecological meaning to the mathematical minimum refinement \(E\vee J\).

## 8. Management changes the state before changing the ecosystem

The action-expansion theorem gives a particularly sharp ecological reading.

A new management option can:

- make a previously unmanageable world viable;
- expose a response difference between worlds previously treated as equivalent;
- force a finer state representation;
- make unchanged monitoring insufficient for full-state identification;
- leave the immediate decision target reportable.

Thus:

> **An intervention does not merely act on an ecological state. By becoming scientifically available, it can change which ecological differences must count as state differences before it is used.**

This is a representational consequence of counterfactual responsibility, not backward causation from the future.

## 9. Dynamical stability versus representational stability

CREST suggests keeping at least two forms of stability separate.

### Dynamical stability

Does the ecological system resist perturbation, recover, remain within a basin, or preserve a relevant dynamical regime?

### Representational stability

Does the same coarse state remain adequate when the scientific contract changes?

A system may be dynamically unchanged while its state representation becomes inadequate because:

- new interventions are considered;
- new colonization routes become possible;
- a latent mechanism becomes decision-relevant;
- a new observation channel reveals a previously collapsed distinction;
- an inherited category is moved into a structurally different system.

The existing action-expansion witness proves one strict case of this separation. A general theory connecting dynamical, evolutionary, and representational stability remains open.

## 10. Ecological rules as quotient laws

Let \(P_V\) be the partition induced by a scientific viewpoint or observation scheme. An effective rule is well-defined on that quotient only if all worlds merged by \(P_V\) agree on the rule's required output/response.

So some ecological rules are best understood as

\[
\boxed{
\text{laws of a scientifically chosen quotient, not necessarily laws of the full latent world.}
}
\]

Changing scale, observation channel, future repertoire, or structural context can make the quotient invalid for the new question without making the original rule false in its original domain.

This provides a non-relativist explanation for why ecological regularities can be robust in one observational context and fail under another.

## 11. One ecology-facing synthesis

The four companions become four questions about ecological sameness:

```text
CCOC — Will a future connection/intervention expose a hidden difference?
MLTR — Does the old category still mean the same after structural change?
MRM  — Do hidden mechanisms agree on the response demanded from the state?
CED  — Has observation actually resolved the distinction the state requires?
```

CREST then asks whether one common state can satisfy all four.

The full ecology-facing claim is therefore:

> **Ecological sameness is a defeasible scientific compression. It is constrained by the futures the system may enter, the meanings inherited from its history, the response mechanisms still considered possible, and the observation channels through which the system is known.**

## 12. Research directions — not yet theorem claims

The current repository should develop only directions that strengthen the one-question spine.

Priority candidates:

1. **snapshot sufficiency:** exact criterion for when a present snapshot factors the full required state;
2. **observation-symmetry obstruction:** characterize when repeated observations from one measurement family can never repay state-identification debt;
3. **trajectory extension:** finite-history or stochastic-response version of the joint-state theorem;
4. **representational resilience:** quantify how much the contract can change before the adequate state must refine;
5. **ecological validation:** worked systems where future, history, mechanism, and evidence distinctions can all be mapped to measurable ecological quantities.

These are future research questions. They should not be converted into new named theorem families until a genuine new proof or empirical discrimination result exists.
