# Ecology-grounded CREST worked case II — fragmented landscapes, extinction debt, and restoration connectivity

> **Status:** literature-grounded conceptual validation, not empirical parameter fitting and not a new theorem. This second case is deliberately outside shallow-lake regime-shift ecology.

## 1. Why this system matters for CREST

Habitat fragmentation provides an unusually direct test of the trajectory-first framing because current landscape pattern can understate the process that created it and the ecological lags that follow.

Recent synthesis explicitly argues that fragmentation should be treated as a process over time rather than inferred only from snapshots of current landscape configuration, emphasizing ecological time lags, extinction debt, colonization credit, and legacies (Fletcher et al. 2026).

Restoration literature also shows that conclusions about fragmentation in remnant habitat are not automatically transferable to restored habitat: restored communities depend strongly on colonization from source populations, dispersal, habitat development, and past land-use legacies (Watts et al. 2024). Colonization credit has been formalized empirically as species yet to colonize restored patches after landscape change (Cristofoli et al. 2010).

This literature **blocks any CREST claim that ecology had not recognized temporal thickness in fragmentation**. CREST's role is instead to map these established processes into an explicit state-adequacy contract.

## 2. Present snapshot

Let a current management snapshot be

\[
X=(\text{patch area},\text{current structural connectivity},\text{current occupancy/species richness}).
\]

Two landscapes or patches can be compatible with the same coarse snapshot while differing in how recently fragmentation or restoration occurred, in source-population access, in dispersal limitation, or in land-use legacy.

This is a conditional state-audit setup, not a claim that every pair with equal current metrics has different futures.

## 3. Temporally extended worlds

### World A — recently fragmented remnant with unpaid extinction debt

A remnant patch can retain species or interactions for some time after fragmentation even though the new area/connectivity regime cannot maintain all of them indefinitely.

Relevant history:

- connectivity was higher in the recent past;
- current richness partly reflects populations or interactions inherited from the pre-fragmentation landscape.

Relevant future-response structure:

- without reconnection or rescue, delayed losses can occur;
- corridor restoration, translocation, or enlargement can alter persistence/recolonization pathways.

### World B — newly restored patch with colonization credit

A restored patch can have apparently suitable current structure yet still lack species because colonization and habitat maturation lag behind habitat creation.

Relevant history:

- suitable habitat was created recently rather than inherited from an older remnant;
- the current community is constrained by source proximity, dispersal ability, establishment, and habitat-development time.

Relevant future-response structure:

- adjacency/corridor creation to source populations can accelerate colonization;
- habitat-quality interventions may matter when establishment rather than dispersal is limiting.

The key point is directional: similar present patch metrics need not carry the same ecological meaning when one patch is a remnant undergoing debt repayment and another is a restored patch accumulating colonization credit.

## 4. CREST mapping

| CREST object | Fragmented-landscape mapping |
|---|---|
| present snapshot \(X\) | current patch area/connectivity and occupancy/species richness |
| relevant history \(h\) | recent vs long-standing fragmentation; remnant vs newly restored origin; past land use |
| future grammar \(\Gamma\) | corridor/reconnection, patch enlargement, habitat creation, translocation/rescue, habitat-quality restoration |
| retained mechanisms \(\Theta\) | demographic persistence/extinction debt; dispersal/source limitation; establishment/habitat-quality limitation; legacy effects |
| inherited semantics \(\mathcal H\) | labels such as “fragmented but occupied”, “restored”, “connected”, or habitat-condition classes carried across conservation vs restoration contexts |
| evidence \(D\) | current occupancy/richness and patch metrics versus historical maps, source-population surveys, dispersal/genetic evidence, demographic trends, habitat-quality/legacy measurements |
| target \(T\) | protect remnant, reconnect, create adjacent habitat, translocate, or improve within-patch habitat quality |

## 5. Snapshot-sufficiency audit

For a purely descriptive target such as

> how many species are currently recorded in this patch?

a current survey can be sufficient.

For a management target such as

> will current occupancy persist, or should restoration prioritize reconnection, source access, or within-patch quality?

the same coarse snapshot can be insufficient when histories and limiting mechanisms imply different futures.

This illustrates the factorization question

\[
X(\omega)=X(\omega')
\centernot\Rightarrow
q_{\mathcal C,V}(\omega)=q_{\mathcal C,V}(\omega')
\]

without treating that implication as a new theorem. The fragmentation literature independently supplies the ecological reason temporal information can matter.

## 6. CCOC reading — future/composition insufficiency

A corridor or restored neighboring patch changes which source populations and dispersal routes become reachable. Under a restricted future with no reconnection, some source/access distinctions may be irrelevant to the management interface. Once reconnection, translocation, or habitat creation becomes available, those distinctions can become operationally important.

This is an ecology-grounded interpretation of CCOC future-grammar expansion. The empirical literature does not prove the CCOC extremal state-memory bound; it supplies a real setting in which the set of accessible future connections is scientifically consequential.

## 7. MLTR reading — direction and inherited semantics

Watts et al. (2024) explicitly argues that fragmentation effects in conserved remnant patches are not automatically transferable to restoration ecology because restored patches are built by colonization processes rather than simply persistence of existing populations.

This makes a particularly clean MLTR example. A label or abstraction learned for a remnant patch can retain its words while losing its operational meaning when transported to a newly restored patch. The source-relative question is whether the inherited classification still groups target patches with the same management-relevant outcomes; if not, it must be refined while preserving valid inherited distinctions.

## 8. MRM reading — low occupancy can have different causes

Low current occupancy in a restored or isolated patch can be compatible with multiple limiting mechanisms:

- inadequate source connectivity / dispersal;
- unsuitable or immature local habitat;
- legacy effects from past land use;
- demographic decline after fragmentation.

These mechanisms need not imply the same response to corridor creation, translocation, or within-patch habitat restoration. Therefore mechanism ambiguity becomes state-relevant only for targets that ask which intervention or future outcome to expect.

## 9. CED reading — current pattern is not mechanism identification

Current patch metrics and occupancy can quantify present pattern without necessarily resolving whether a missing species reflects dispersal limitation, establishment limitation, historical debt, or unsuitable local conditions.

That is an inference from the distinct temporal and mechanistic processes documented in the literature, not a universal statement about every monitoring programme.

CREST therefore separates:

- the **required state distinction** for a management target;
- the **identified state** supported by actual historical, demographic, dispersal, genetic, and habitat evidence;
- targets that remain reportable despite unresolved mechanism/history.

## 10. Strong novelty-control consequence

This case creates an important negative result for CREST positioning:

> **“Current snapshot is insufficient because fragmentation is a temporal process” is not a CREST novelty.**

Fletcher et al. (2026) makes essentially this ecological diagnosis directly for fragmentation.

The CREST contribution must therefore remain one level higher and more structured: given known temporal/legacy processes, which differences must a particular scientific state retain under specified future, semantic, mechanistic, evidence, and target responsibilities?

## 11. What this second case adds

Together with the shallow-lake case, CREST now has two heterogeneous ecology-grounded mappings:

1. **shallow lakes:** alternative regimes, sediment nutrient legacy, food-web/macrophyte feedback, restoration channels;
2. **fragmented landscapes:** extinction debt, colonization credit, dispersal/source limitation, habitat-development and land-use legacy, corridor/restoration actions.

The shared CREST structure survives despite very different ecological mechanisms. This strengthens the generality argument at the conceptual-mapping level.

It still does not establish empirical predictive superiority over mechanistic models, PSRs, POMDPs, or existing restoration frameworks.
