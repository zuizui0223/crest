# CREST restoration/conservation prior-art positioning — 2026-08-24

> Purpose: prevent the restoration/conservation empirical mainline from turning known restoration concepts into CREST novelty claims.

## Nearest neighbours that must be credited

### 1. Society for Ecological Restoration standards

The 2026 SER International Principles and Standards already separate baseline assessment, goals, objectives, implementation, measurable indicators, effectiveness/performance monitoring, impact monitoring and adaptive management. DOI: `10.1111/rec.70441`.

Therefore CREST does **not** claim novelty for:

- separating restoration actions from restoration outcomes;
- distinguishing goals/objectives from monitoring indicators;
- updating monitoring through adaptive management;
- requiring intervention/control comparisons where appropriate.

CREST's narrower question is whether the intervention changes which distinctions must belong to the **state representation itself**.

### 2. Ecological memory, history and legacy

Restoration ecology already treats historical contingency and ecological memory as central.

- Balaguer et al. 2014 explicitly develop restoration reference systems from latent and active ecological memory and argue that ecosystems are historically contingent and multilayered. DOI: `10.1016/j.biocon.2014.05.007`.
- Higgs et al. 2014 emphasize historical knowledge, ecological legacies and the choices available to practitioners under open-ended change. DOI: `10.1890/110267`.
- Case & Hallett 2021 distinguish multiple meanings of history and argue that historical events shape restoration goals and trajectories. DOI: `10.1111/rec.13411`.

Therefore CREST does **not** claim that it discovered that restoration outcomes depend on history, legacy, path dependence or hidden ecological memory.

The CREST contribution is conditional and representational: **a historical distinction must enter the scientific state exactly when forgetting it merges worlds that disagree on a response, meaning or target required by the declared contract.**

### 3. State-and-Transition Models and threshold frameworks

State-and-Transition Models (STMs) are a major nearest neighbour.

Stringham, Krueger & Shaver (2003) organize ecosystem responses to natural and management-induced disturbance in terms of states, transitions and thresholds. DOI: `10.2307/4003893`.

Briske, Fuhlendorf & Smeins (2005) explicitly allow multiple states and management prescriptions in state-transition thinking and distinguish structural from functional threshold components.

Bestelmeyer (2006) warns that overly simple classification thresholds can mislead management and argues for broader state attributes. DOI: `10.1111/j.1526-100X.2006.00140.x`.

Most importantly for the new empirical arc, Luxton et al. (2026) explicitly use STMs as a contextual framework for leading indicators of restoration trajectories, emphasize starting states, and incorporate restoration interventions as drivers of transitions. DOI: `10.1111/2041-210X.70164`.

Therefore CREST does **not** claim novelty for:

- representing restoration as movement among ecological states;
- allowing management interventions to drive transitions;
- using starting states to condition restoration outcomes;
- selecting leading/lagging indicators for restoration monitoring;
- using thresholds or alternative states in restoration.

### 4. Restoration as an experimental test of ecological theory

Restoration ecology has long been presented as an unusually strong experimental setting for testing ecological understanding. Young, Petersen & Clary (2005) describe restoration as an "acid test" of ecological theory and review succession, assembly, state-transition, contingency, seed limitation and soil processes. DOI: `10.1111/j.1461-0248.2005.00764.x`.

CREST therefore should not present restoration as a newly discovered proving ground for ecology.

# What remains distinctive in CREST

The safe restoration-facing claim is **not**:

> restoration actions change ecological states.

It is also **not**:

> historical legacies alter restoration outcomes.

And it is **not**:

> monitoring must be adapted to the restoration goal.

The narrower CREST architecture is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{feasible ecological worlds}
\rightarrow
\text{contract-required state}
\rightarrow
\text{evidence-identified state}
\rightarrow
\text{reportable target}.
}
\]

These layers are separated rather than folded into one management state.

The theorem-level claim remains the connected finite separation:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta|K^*|=1,
\qquad
\Delta K_{U_0}=m
}
\]

for arbitrary finite `m`, together with fixed-evidence full-state licensing loss and coarse-target retention.

The restoration cases then support a qualitative empirical motif:

\[
\boxed{
\text{new restoration capability}
\rightarrow
\text{new response-relevant distinctions}
\rightarrow
\text{required-state refinement}
\rightarrow
\text{possible monitoring inadequacy}
}
\]

while a coarser management target can remain reportable.

The empirical motif is compatible with, and partially anticipated by, existing restoration theory. What is new in CREST is the **cross-gate formal separation and no-bound construction**, not the individual ecological ingredients.

# Direct comparison with STM/restoration monitoring

| Question | STM / restoration standards | CREST |
|---|---|---|
| What ecosystem state/trajectory is relevant? | explicit states, transitions, starting states, thresholds | possible-world quotient under a declared contract |
| Can management drive transitions? | yes | yes; not novel |
| Does history matter? | yes | yes; not novel |
| Which indicators monitor recovery? | explicit leading/lagging/performance/impact indicators | evidence partition `E_D` kept separate from required state `J` |
| Can a goal be reported without full state? | operationally often yes | formalized as target factorization through evidence |
| Does added intervention capability change required representation? | can be represented contextually | made an explicit state-adequacy question |
| Is representational burden bounded by ecological capability gain? | no comparable general claim identified in targeted audit | connected finite no-bound theorem |

The last two rows are the safe novelty-bearing interface. A database-complete systematic review would still be required for historical-firstness.

# Safe manuscript wording

> Restoration ecology already recognizes historical contingency, alternative states, management-induced transitions, purpose-specific indicators and adaptive monitoring. CREST does not claim these ideas as new. Its contribution is to separate the ecological worlds made feasible by intervention, the state resolution required by a declared scientific responsibility, the state resolution actually identified by evidence, and the target that remains reportable; its finite construction then shows that a fixed-size expansion of future capability can have arbitrarily larger consequences for required and monitored state resolution.

## Blocked wording

Do not write:

- "CREST shows for the first time that restoration depends on history."
- "CREST introduces intervention-dependent ecological states."
- "CREST is the first framework to connect states and restoration management."
- "CREST discovers that monitoring requirements depend on restoration goals."
- "CREST proves that real restoration projects have unbounded monitoring cost."

The last statement is especially important: the no-bound result is a finite existence theorem, while the restoration systems provide independent qualitative projections only.
