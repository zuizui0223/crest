# Primary-source audit of the restoration/conservation cross-domain claim — 2026-08-24

## Audit question and decision rule

This audit treats the claims in `docs/restoration_conservation_cross_domain_validation_2026-08-24.md` and `manuscript/restoration_conservation_examples_insert.md` as hypotheses. It asks whether primary evidence supports the following three-part empirical motif in four independent restoration domains:

1. admitting an intervention makes distinctions that could previously be collapsed relevant to the response;
2. a monitoring design that is adequate for a narrower question may not identify those distinctions; and
3. a coarser management or effectiveness target may nevertheless remain reportable.

The primary studies did not formulate or test CREST's quotient condition. Accordingly, this audit distinguishes:

- **direct observation** — a study measured the intervention, distinction, monitoring limitation, or target;
- **cross-study support** — complementary primary studies establish different links of the motif;
- **CREST interpretation** — the mapping from those observations to `required state`, `identified state`, or `reportable target` is ours rather than the authors' result.

The Society for Ecological Restoration (SER) third-edition Standards are used only as an official practice standard. They distinguish implementation/compliance, effectiveness/performance, and impact monitoring; require goals, objectives, measurable indicators, baselines, and treatment/control comparisons where appropriate; and require reporting to specify the monitoring basis of an evaluation. They do **not** state or prove CREST's four-layer result. Source: [Gann et al. 2026, DOI 10.1111/rec.70441](https://doi.org/10.1111/rec.70441).

## Cross-case verdict

| Case | Intervention makes a distinction response-relevant | Narrow monitoring may miss it | Coarser target remains reportable | Overall verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dam removal | Direct for species, migration timing, spatial extent, and resident/anadromous life-history form | Direct for the Elwha presence/nondetection eDNA design | Direct for fish passage/connectivity of focal species | **Strongest complete case**, although the pre-intervention act of collapsing states is a CREST interpretation |
| Invasive-plant removal | Direct for removal method, functional group, site degradation, seed bank, and soil legacy in particular systems | Direct but not universal; above-ground vegetation sometimes tracks seed banks and sometimes misses components | Direct for invader reduction versus native-community recovery | **Strong recurrence with explicit counterexamples to universality** |
| Peatland rewetting | Direct for vegetation/substrate, peat type, hydrology, and disturbance legacy as response modifiers | Direct for water-table or community-composition proxies versus process recovery | Direct for hydrological rewetting; climate-function reporting remains much stronger and unresolved | **Strong functional mismatch; only moderate support for the full causal chain** |
| Prescribed fire | Direct for pre-fire ecological condition, duff moisture/consumption, and cryptic plant persistence | Direct across complementary studies; not all in a single first-entry-burn experiment | Fuel reduction or post-burn richness can be reported while mechanism/long-term state remains unresolved | **Independent support, but the full motif is assembled across systems and timescales** |

The defensible cross-domain statement is therefore:

> Across four restoration domains, primary studies show that interventions can expose response-relevant ecological heterogeneity, that monitoring sufficient for a narrower implementation or presence question may not resolve that heterogeneity, and that a coarser target can still be reported. The exact claim that a newly admitted future capability *causes a required-state refinement* is a CREST interpretation of these results, not a proposition tested by the ecological studies.

The stronger sentence in the current validation document — that the qualitative cross-gate motif simply “occurs independently” in all four domains — needs this qualification. None of the studies compared a deliberately coarse pre-intervention quotient against a response-aware quotient, and none establishes the finite theorem's one-capability/arbitrary-burden result.

## R1 — Dam removal

### What the primary evidence directly shows

The Elwha studies provide unusually close support for all three empirical components.

- Removal of the two dams restored access to about 130 km of habitat. Multi-year radiotelemetry showed Bull Trout rapidly entered newly accessible headwaters, increased their upstream extent and distance annually, and expressed river–estuary and likely marine movements after reconnection. [Brenkman et al. 2019, DOI 10.1002/nafm.10291](https://doi.org/10.1002/nafm.10291).
- Stable-isotope evidence showed that a Bull Trout population that had been almost entirely landlocked for a century rapidly resumed anadromy after dam removal. This directly establishes that life-history form became relevant to interpreting the response to reconnection. [Quinn et al. 2017, DOI 10.1007/s10641-017-0676-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10641-017-0676-0).
- Duda et al. sampled 25 sites in 33 of 40 possible months during 2014–2017 with 11 species-specific assays (eight newly developed and three existing). Most targeted anadromous species passed both former dam sites, but timing and spatial extent differed among species. More abundant Chinook and Coho reached farther upstream than less abundant Pink and Chum salmon. [Duda et al. 2021, DOI 10.1002/edn3.134](https://doi.org/10.1002/edn3.134).
- The same Duda study explicitly states that its eDNA response was presence/nondetection only and therefore provided limited information about population dynamics. Its assays also could not distinguish resident from anadromous forms of Bull Trout and Rainbow Trout/Steelhead; radiotelemetry and stable isotopes detected important migration and life-history responses that the eDNA assays could not attribute.
- Duda et al. nevertheless state that detecting focal fish upstream showed that key project objectives — fish passage and restored connectivity — were being met for several species. This is a direct example of a coarse effectiveness target remaining answerable while population size, reproductive establishment, and life-history state remain unresolved.

### CREST interpretation

Before passage was possible, `present above or below the barrier`, species identity, timing, and resident/anadromous form did not all need to be retained for the narrow prediction `can this fish pass the dam?`: passage was blocked. After removal, those distinctions separated recolonization responses. This is a close real-world analogue of an action making previously collapsible distinctions response-relevant.

### Negative findings and claim ceiling

- The eDNA study supports a limitation of **that presence/nondetection assay design**, not a categorical failure of eDNA or “old monitoring.” Quantitative eDNA, telemetry, genetics, redd counts, and other methods answer different questions.
- Detection above a former dam does not establish abundance, successful reproduction, a self-sustaining population, stock identity, direct ancestry, or full-community recovery.
- `Stock identity`, gene flow, and food-web trajectories in the current mapping are plausible post-removal concerns but were not identified by Duda et al.'s assays. They should not be presented as direct results of that study.
- The studies observe responses after removal; they do not experimentally show that managers or scientists actually used a coarser state before removal.

**Case verdict:** retain as the primary empirical anchor. It directly supports feasible-future expansion, action-relevant life-history/species distinctions, a method-specific identification gap, and a separately reportable passage target. Keep the required-state language explicitly interpretive.

## R2 — Invasive-plant removal

### What the primary evidence directly shows

Three field experiments support different parts of the motif.

- In eight invaded Indiana forest sites (320 plots), Flory and Clay compared hand-weeding and two herbicide strategies for *Microstegium vimineum*. The companion effectiveness results reported in the paper showed 99% biomass reduction under both herbicide strategies and 87% under hand-weeding, but native responses differed among removal methods and functional groups. Hand-weeding and post-emergent herbicide increased native diversity by 24% and 21%, respectively, whereas adding a pre-emergent herbicide produced no positive diversity response and inhibited native return. The authors could not compare the recovered community with the pre-invasion community and cautioned that the study did not establish long-term response. [Flory & Clay 2009, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2664.2009.01610.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2664.2009.01610.x).
- Across six Great Salt Lake wetland sites, Rohal et al. applied six *Phragmites australis* treatments for three years and monitored above-ground vegetation and germinable seed banks for two additional years. Baseline seed-bank communities differed strongly with site degradation; herbicide reduced *Phragmites* seed density, but native seed banks were insufficient for recovery at more degraded sites. Above-ground vegetation **generally reflected** seed-bank communities, yet native graminoids occurred in seed banks while remaining rare above ground. [Rohal et al. 2021, DOI 10.1111/avsc.12626](https://doi.org/10.1111/avsc.12626).
- After removing above-ground Japanese knotweed biomass from a >20-year invasion, Zhang et al. manipulated below-ground legacy with biochar and activated carbon. Prairie-species biomass was 80% higher in amended than unamended plots and soil/microbial measures changed consistently with altered phenolic and nutrient processes. However, the seeded native species did **not** respond positively to the carbon amendments. [Zhang et al. 2021, DOI 10.1111/1365-2664.13757](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2664.13757).

These experiments jointly show that `invader reduced` and `native community recovered` are different questions. Once recovery after removal is the task, removal method, site degradation, seed-bank sufficiency, functional group, and particular soil legacies can distinguish response trajectories.

### Field-level monitoring prevalence — systematic review, not primary causal evidence

Singh and Byun's systematic review reports that more than 35% of included studies described reinvasion, secondary invasion, or both, while only 6.5% investigated native and invasive seed-bank responses after removal. This is useful evidence that a potentially relevant below-ground dimension is rarely measured; it is not an experiment and must not carry an `A` primary-evidence grade. [Singh & Byun 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.ecoleng.2023.107122](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoleng.2023.107122).

### CREST interpretation

For the target `above-ground invader reduced`, above-ground cover or biomass can be sufficient. For the target `native community recovers`, treatment selectivity, seed-bank sufficiency, propagule pressure, and some soil legacies may need to enter the predictive state. The required refinement is target- and system-specific, not a universal list of below-ground variables.

### Negative findings and claim ceiling

- Rohal et al. found that above-ground vegetation **generally did reflect** seed-bank communities. The evidence therefore rejects a categorical claim that above-ground monitoring cannot identify seed-bank state; it shows specific mismatches and degraded-site insufficiency.
- Zhang et al.'s amendments benefited the prairie-species mixture but not the seeded native species. The experiment does not support the broad claim that measuring or treating a soil legacy will generally restore native flora.
- Flory and Clay could not determine similarity to the pre-invasion community and did not establish long-term recovery. Successful control and short-term positive native response are both narrower than restoration to a reference state.
- Neither of the two reviews currently used as main anchors — Nsikani et al. 2018 or Singh and Byun 2023 — is primary experimental evidence. The current `A/B` evidence grade based on “large reviews plus long-term removal experiments” is too coarse; identify the experiment used for each direct claim.

**Case verdict:** retain as the best evidence/target-separation example, but anchor it in Flory and Clay, Rohal et al., and Zhang et al. Use the systematic review only for monitoring prevalence. Replace `above-ground monitoring leaves the below-ground state unresolved` with `above-ground control monitoring can leave response-relevant seed-bank or soil dimensions unresolved in some systems`.

## R3 — Peatland rewetting

### What the primary evidence directly shows

- Urbanová and Bárta compared long-drained, 7–16-year rewetted, and undrained bogs and spruce swamp forests and added natural substrates in process assays. Rewetting brought methanogen abundance and community composition close to pristine conditions, but methane-production potential remained as low as at drained sites. Substrate addition restored production toward pristine levels, implicating decomposed peat, limited substrate supply, and slow return of original vegetation. [Urbanová & Bárta 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.ecoleng.2020.105852](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoleng.2020.105852).
- In a 4,800-ha Sumatra trial monitored for 7.5 years, 257 peat dams raised water tables from about −0.6 m to −0.3 m and halved subsidence rates. The study also found 57 native peat-swamp-forest tree species growing spontaneously in the most rewetted conditions. This is a positive multi-dimensional outcome, not evidence that vegetation recovery failed. The authors state that limited pre-intervention data prevented a full comparison with initial plantation conditions. [Hooijer et al. 2024, DOI 10.1038/s41598-024-60462-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-60462-3).
- A continental comparison of 320 rewetted and 243 near-natural European fen sites found long-lasting differences in vegetation, geochemistry, hydrology, and remotely sensed land-cover characteristics. Sixty-three percent of rewetted sites lay outside the near-natural 95% confidence ellipse for vegetation, versus 20% for hydrology. This is strong comparative evidence that hydrological resemblance and biodiversity recovery are not interchangeable, but it is not a randomized before–after intervention. [Kreyling et al. 2021, DOI 10.1038/s41467-021-25619-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-25619-y).

### CREST interpretation

The Urbanová and Bárta results directly reject the substitution `rewetted/anaerobic conditions` or even `methanogen composition near pristine` for `methanogenic process recovered`. Under a methane-process target, vegetation-derived substrate and peat condition are response-relevant. Hydrological rewetting remains measurable as a coarser target.

### Negative findings, attribution correction, and claim ceiling

- **The current manuscript attribution is wrong.** DOI `10.1016/j.ecoleng.2020.105852` is by **Zuzana Urbanová and Jiří Bárta**, not “Juottonen et al. 2020.” Juottonen authored a different 2020 reciprocal-transplant paper (DOI `10.1111/1365-2435.13536`).
- Urbanová and Bárta measured methanogenic community properties and **methane-production potential**, not ecosystem net greenhouse-gas balance, carbon sequestration, or radiative forcing. Their study supports `hydrology/community composition does not identify methane-process recovery`; it does not directly establish that `climate function` was unrecovered.
- Hooijer et al. found both reduced subsidence and spontaneous native-tree regrowth. It supports the need to report hydrology, subsidence, and vegetation separately, but not a claim that one succeeded while the other failed.
- Kreyling et al. compared rewetted and near-natural sites across space and restoration ages; site selection and history were not randomized. It demonstrates persistent multi-attribute difference, not the response of matched worlds to one identical action.

**Case verdict:** retain as a strong target-relativity and proxy/function mismatch. Correct the authorship immediately. Lower the claim from `carbon-climate function remains unresolved` to `methane-process or multi-attribute recovery remains unresolved unless carbon-balance evidence is added`.

## R4 — Prescribed-fire reintroduction

### What the primary evidence directly shows

The prescribed-fire case has three useful primary anchors, but they answer different parts of the motif.

- In a six-year California prairie restoration experiment, Young et al. burned a random half of 19 plots after four subordinate species and one formerly dominant species had declined to zero or near-zero above-ground cover. Burning caused three nearly absent subordinate species to reappear, reduced dominant grasses, and increased richness and Shannon diversity. The study is a clean demonstration that a zero/near-zero above-ground observation did not imply an inability to respond to fire. However, the authors did not measure the seed bank and explicitly allowed both competitive release and fire germination cues as explanations. [Young et al. 2015, DOI 10.1890/ES14-00303.1](https://doi.org/10.1890/ES14-00303.1).
- Varner et al. assigned 16 approximately 40-year fire-excluded, 10-ha longleaf-pine stands to wet, moist, dry, or no-burn treatments based on duff moisture. Dry burns produced 20.5% mean overstory mortality, whereas wet and moist burns did not differ from controls. Duff consumption and crown scorch were the best individual-tree mortality predictors; lower-duff moisture explained 78% of duff-consumption variation. The paper calls duff consumption an often overlooked effect. [Varner et al. 2007, DOI 10.1139/X06-315](https://doi.org/10.1139/X06-315).
- A 20-year experimental forest-management study found that repeated prescribed burning reduced soil-seed-bank diversity and abundance even though standing-vegetation diversity had increased with frequent fire. This directly demonstrates that above- and below-ground monitoring can license different conclusions. It concerns repeated fire rather than first-entry fire. [Penman et al. 2011, DOI 10.1111/j.1442-9993.2010.02192.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2010.02192.x).
- In mountain big sagebrush, spring and fall burns produced different seed-bank responses across cheatgrass-, native-, and juniper-dominated ecological conditions. For example, spring fire reduced immediate cheatgrass germination by 91% and 86% at cheatgrass- and native-dominated sites, respectively, but the reduction did not persist one year later; native dicots increased strongly at the native-dominated site. Pre-fire ecological condition therefore modified the response to the same intervention class. [Faist et al. 2013, DOI 10.1016/j.jaridenv.2013.04.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaridenv.2013.04.001).

### CREST interpretation

Once `burn` becomes a supported future action, above-ground presence alone can be too coarse for predicting which species reappear, and stand appearance or nominal fire history can be too coarse for predicting duff-mediated mortality. The measurable treatment or fuel-reduction result can remain reportable while mechanism, regeneration potential, or long-term resilience remains unresolved.

### Negative findings and claim ceiling

- Young et al. did not identify dormant seeds directly. `Cryptic persistence` is consistent with, but not uniquely identified by, the experiment; competitive release is an alternative.
- Duff moisture is not inherently unobservable. Varner et al. measured it successfully. The warranted claim is that a conventional or incomplete state description may omit it, not that monitoring cannot identify it.
- Penman et al. concern repeated prescribed burns over 20 years, while Varner et al. concern first-entry fire after long exclusion. Combining them supports recurrence, not a single experimentally demonstrated chain.
- `Burn completed` is implementation monitoring, and `fuel reduced` is a narrow effectiveness measure. Neither should be described as ecological restoration success without the corresponding recovery indicators.
- Varner et al. 2005 (DOI `10.1111/j.1526-100X.2005.00067.x`) is a review of case studies, not the primary experiment. The current `A/B` evidence grade should instead cite Varner et al. 2007 and specify which link is supported.

**Case verdict:** retain as independent support. It gives the clearest visual example of an apparently absent above-ground component responding after fire and strong primary evidence for action-sensitive duff and seed-bank distinctions. Present the monitoring and coarse-target links as cross-study support, not as one fully observed causal chain.

## Required corrections to the current validation and insert

1. Replace `Juottonen et al. 2020` for DOI `10.1016/j.ecoleng.2020.105852` with `Urbanová & Bárta 2020`.
2. Do not label Varner et al. 2005 or the invasive-plant reviews as primary experiments. Use Varner et al. 2007, Flory and Clay 2009, Rohal et al. 2021, and Zhang et al. 2021 for direct claims; retain reviews only for synthesis or monitoring prevalence.
3. Revise evidence grades so they describe the exact design: Elwha is multi-method post-removal monitoring rather than a controlled before/after experiment; Urbanová and Bárta combine field comparison with substrate-addition assays; Kreyling et al. are a large comparative dataset; Hooijer et al. have limited pre-intervention data; the fire motif spans different experiments.
4. Narrow `eDNA identifies only part of the response structure` to the documented limitations of the Elwha presence/nondetection assays. Do not generalize to all eDNA designs.
5. Narrow `above-ground vegetation monitoring leaves below-ground recovery unresolved` to a conditional statement. Rohal et al. found general above-/below-ground agreement with specific mismatches.
6. Do not use Hooijer et al. as evidence of failed vegetation recovery: they observed 57 native tree species and dense spontaneous regrowth in the most rewetted conditions.
7. Do not infer net climate recovery from methane-production potential. Add direct ecosystem carbon/GHG evidence or cap the claim at methane-process recovery.
8. Keep ongoing Klamath monitoring prospective. An active project description is not final empirical evidence for the cross-domain causal claim.

## Final claim ceiling

The four cases justify a manuscript-level **existence and recurrence** claim with graded wording:

> Primary studies from dam removal, invasive-plant removal, peatland rewetting, and prescribed fire are consistent with the same qualitative architecture: an intervention can expose response-relevant distinctions that a narrower monitoring design does not resolve, even when a coarser implementation or effectiveness target remains reportable.

They do not justify any of the following stronger claims:

- every restoration intervention necessarily increases required-state resolution;
- the same monitoring gap occurs in every site or method;
- the ecological studies identify the minimal sufficient state;
- a one-action change produces a quantified or unbounded state burden;
- reporting an implementation or focal-species target establishes ecosystem recovery; or
- the four cases independently prove the CREST theorem.
