# Manuscript insert — restoration and conservation as the primary ecological arc

> Intended role: replace island/urban as the primary empirical illustration. These cases do not prove the finite CREST theorem; they show that its qualitative carrier/state/evidence/target separation recurs across independent restoration domains.

## Restoration changes what must be known

Ecological restoration provides a particularly direct setting for CREST because restoration is explicitly intervention-driven. A degraded ecosystem can admit one coarse description while a management action is unavailable, yet require a finer state once dam removal, invasive-species control, rewetting, prescribed burning, reintroduction, or another intervention becomes part of the future the representation must support. The key empirical question is not simply whether treatment effects are heterogeneous. It is whether worlds treated as the same before intervention diverge under the same newly relevant action.

The 2026 Society for Ecological Restoration standards already separate baseline assessment, goals and objectives, implementation, indicators, effectiveness monitoring, impact monitoring, and adaptive management (Gann et al. 2026). CREST does not claim novelty for those distinctions. It adds a state-representation question: **does the intervention make a previously erased ecological difference necessary for prediction, monitoring, or reporting?**

### Dam removal: feasible futures expand before full recovery is known

The Elwha River is a strong example of the first CREST layer. Two dams had blocked anadromous fish from more than 90% of the watershed. Their removal reopened upstream habitat and therefore enlarged the set of ecologically feasible migration and recolonization trajectories. Post-removal eDNA monitoring using 11 species-specific assays showed that most targeted anadromous species moved above both former dam sites, but recolonization timing and spatial extent differed among species (Duda et al. 2021). Thus `barrier removed` is not itself an adequate post-restoration ecological state. Once passage is possible, species identity, life history, migration timing and habitat response can become distinctions that a predictive state must retain. Nevertheless, a coarse target such as whether a focal salmon species has passed a former barrier can remain reportable without complete identification of the restored community state.

### Invasive-species control: a target can be achieved while restoration state remains unresolved

Invasive-plant removal makes the evidence/target distinction especially visible. Large reviews show that above-ground control can leave persistent soil and seed-bank legacies, including altered microbial communities, elevated nutrients, depleted native seed banks, secondary invasion and reinvasion. A 440-study review identified these effects as barriers to restoration after control of invasive N-fixing woody plants (Nsikani et al. 2018), while a later systematic review found reinvasion or secondary invasion in more than one third of the studies it examined and emphasized invasive seed banks and external propagule supply (2023, DOI `10.1016/j.ecoleng.2023.107122`). The management statement `the invader was successfully controlled` can therefore be licensed by existing monitoring while the stronger statement `the native ecosystem has recovered` remains unresolved. CREST treats these as different report targets rather than two confidence levels for one state.

### Peatland rewetting: the same hydrological action can require a different state for a different target

Peatland rewetting shows why restoration state is target-relative. Long-term rewetting can restore methanogen abundance and community composition toward pristine conditions while methane-production activity remains limited because original vegetation and substrate supply have not recovered (Juottonen et al. 2020). A large-scale tropical peatland trial likewise showed that raising water tables reduced peat subsidence, while forest regeneration remained a separate recovery dimension. Hydrological success therefore does not automatically identify biogeochemical or climate-function recovery. A state sufficient for the target `water table restored` can be too coarse for the target `carbon-cycle function restored`.

### Independent recurrence in prescribed fire

Fire restoration provides an independent replication of the same motif. Reintroducing fire after long exclusion can expose distinctions that were not represented in an above-ground vegetation snapshot: accumulated duff and fuels, time since fire, soil seed bank, invasive load and ecological condition. In long-unburned longleaf-pine systems, first-entry burns have produced unexpected old-tree mortality associated with altered fuel and duff structure (Varner et al. 2005). Reviews and experiments also show that prescribed-fire effects on seed banks can differ from the visible vegetation response. Thus adding `burn` to the future grammar can make historical and below-ground differences state-relevant before the fire is actually applied.

Across freshwater restoration, invasion management, peatland restoration and fire ecology, the repeated structure is therefore

\[
\boxed{
\text{new restoration capability}
\rightarrow
\text{new response-relevant distinctions}
\rightarrow
\text{finer required state}
\rightarrow
\text{possible monitoring inadequacy},
}
\]

while a coarser management target can remain reportable. The ecological cases do not establish the arbitrary finite scaling in the CREST construction. Their role is different: they show that the qualitative cross-gate motif occurs independently in restoration domains with different organisms, mechanisms, measurements and management actions. The finite theorem then shows why no carrier-gain-only bound on representational burden should be expected without additional structural assumptions.
